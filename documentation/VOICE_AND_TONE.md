# Voice and tone: how Kevin sounds

The reference for anything written as Kevin Tambo or OKJTechnologies:
Insights articles, site copy, proposals, emails. Read it before writing.

## Who's speaking

A compassionate, people-centred builder. Kevin sees the people a system
serves before the system itself: the person who carries the load, the work
that goes unthanked, the cost someone pays quietly. He's an optimist and he
means it practically: **no problem is impossible**, there's only a problem
that hasn't been mapped properly yet.

## What the voice does

- **Starts with people.** Before the stack or the feature: who is this for,
  what are they carrying, what would make their day lighter?
- **Notices the unseen.** It names the work that usually goes unthanked:
  the receptionist re-typing records, the rider who doesn't know their
  rights, the client who has to run the system after launch. Then it honours it.
- **Is warm without being soft.** Direct, sure of itself, honest. Empathy
  that also says the hard thing when it needs saying.
- **Is optimistic and grounded.** It rejects scarcity thinking ("that's just
  how it is here") and points to a way through. The hope is always tied to a
  concrete next step.
- **Is honest about where things stand.** Live is live, in development is in
  development, a roadmap is a roadmap. No invented numbers, no invented
  quotes, no invented clients. Hope never stands in for fact.
- **Gives credit.** Partners, clients, colleagues and teachers get named and
  thanked. The work is rarely one person's alone.

## How it sounds on the page

- **Keep the focus on the work, not on Kevin.** Case studies, service pages
  and site copy speak about the work, with **OKJTechnologies** as the subject
  when someone has to act ("OKJTechnologies built…"), never "I". This makes it
  easy to say the studio contributed to something larger (a Lawyers Hub
  programme, a partner's product) without shrinking the work to one person.
  Never invent a "team" or "we" for the studio either; "we" only for genuinely
  shared work (with a client, a partner, Lawyers Hub colleagues).
- Articles follow the same rule: the work and the people lead, no "I". Where
  Kevin's own role matters (the Lawyers Hub years, the hackathon, the coding
  class), name it as "OKJTechnologies' founder". The byline is OKJTechnologies.
- Plain words a non-technical reader can follow. A technical term earns its
  place by being explained in one clause.
- Rhythm: mostly measured sentences, then a short one that lands.
  *"A big system is just a lot of small, kind decisions."*
- Speaks to the reader as a peer: "you", not "users" or "stakeholders"
  in the abstract.
- Ends forward: with what becomes possible, an invitation, or encouragement.

## Words and moves to avoid

- Hype and filler: "revolutionary", "cutting-edge", "seamless", "leverage",
  "synergy", "world-class".
- Cynicism and scarcity framing: "no good deed goes unpunished", "it can't
  be done here".
- Blame. Name what went wrong, not who to blame for it.
- Unverifiable stats or testimonials (see the framing rules in
  `PROJECT_CATALOG_ACCURATE.md`).

## A quick test

Read the draft back and ask:

1. Can I see a real person in it, and do they come out of it respected?
2. Is every claim true today?
3. Does it leave the reader feeling that their problem can be solved?
